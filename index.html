t-SNE: Visualising High‑Dimensional Data

PCA couldn't separate SwiftParcel's lookalike digits. Here's how t-SNE turns distances into neighbour probabilities, fixes the "crowding problem," and builds maps where similar things sit together — and how to read those maps without being misled by them.

The Map That Wouldn't Separate

Last week, Meera at SwiftParcel used PCA to shrink handwritten PIN-code scans from 784 numbers to a few dozen, and drew a 2D map of the digits. The reaction from the operations head was mixed:

  • "Good, the 0s and 1s are clearly separated."
  • "But 4s, 7s and 9s are sitting on top of each other. If this is what the data looks like, I can't tell whether the hubs' scanners are producing distinct digit shapes or just noise."

The operations team wants a map where similar-looking scans sit together as visible islands, to spot unusual scanner batches and handwriting styles. There are still no labels.

Meera realises the problem. PCA looks for the single direction of greatest overall spread — it cares about the big picture. What the operations team wants is a map that respects who is near whom: the local neighbourhoods.

That is exactly what t-SNE does. Today: how it works, why it can unravel what PCA cannot, and how it can mislead you if you read the map the wrong way.

Section 1

1PCA Recap: Three Questions

PCA finds new axes (principal components) that are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered by how much variance they capture.

Think first

If we have 5 PCs, what is the angle between any two of them?

Eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix (like the covariance matrix) are orthogonal, so the angle is always 90 degrees.

Think first

Is there multicollinearity after PCA?

No. The PCs are perpendicular, so the dot product between any two of them is zero — the transformed features are uncorrelated. This is a real advantage of PCA.

Think first

PCA has assumptions. What are they?

1. Features are correlated. If features are uncorrelated, variance is spread evenly across the originals — every PC captures a similar amount, so there's little to compress.

2. Features are on comparable scales. PCA chases variance, and features with big numbers have big variance, so they dominate the first PC. Standardise first.

Seeing the scale assumption in action

Weight (kg, 40–120) and height (cm, 150–200) — both uniformly spread, but on very different numeric ranges.

Quick check

A teammate says: "After PCA, my features are perpendicular, so I no longer have multicollinearity, and I can skip standardising because PCA is scale-free." What is right and what is wrong?

Correct. PCs are orthogonal, so the transformed features are uncorrelated and multicollinearity is gone. But PCA is not scale-free — it maximises variance, so features with larger numeric ranges dominate unless you standardise first.
Section 2

2Where PCA Falls Short

Reproducing the problem: two clusters PCA merges

Two clearly separate groups — widely spread along x, but separated only along y. What happens when PCA reduces them to one dimension?

PCA picked the x-direction, because that's where the data spreads most. Projecting onto it makes the two groups overlap completely. The structure that mattered — the small gap along y — lived in a direction with less variance, so PCA threw it away.

Global vs. local structure

  • Global structure: the overall shape and spread of the entire dataset. PCA preserves this.
  • Local structure: who is near whom — the neighbourhoods. PCA can destroy this.

For visualising clusters and neighbourhoods, we want a method that protects local structure.

Section 3

3t-SNE: The Intuition

t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) is built on one idea:

If two points are close neighbours in the high-dimensional space, they should be close neighbours in the low-dimensional map.

Think of a school photo. You don't try to keep every distance between every pair of students exactly right — impossible on a flat page. You only make sure friends who stand together in real life stand together in the photo.

Take a point A with close neighbours B and C. t-SNE places A, B, and C near each other in 2D. It also keeps points that were far from A relatively far away, but it cares much more about getting the close relationships right.

Note the careful wording: t-SNE preserves neighbourhoods (who is near whom), not exact distances. This will matter when we read maps.

Quick check

What is the core idea of t-SNE when reducing dimensions?

Correct. t-SNE builds a low-dimensional map in which local neighbourhoods match those of the original data. Maximising variance is PCA's goal — the other options don't describe any dimensionality-reduction objective.

4See It Work First: PCA vs. t-SNE on Meera's Digits

Practical recipe

For t-SNE on high-dimensional data: first reduce with PCA to ~50 dimensions (fast, removes noise), then run t-SNE to 2D. This is standard practice, because t-SNE's cost grows quickly with the number of points and dimensions.

    a.set_title(title)

  • t-SNE (right): digits form distinct islands, even though t-SNE never saw the labels.
  • Islands that sit close together (e.g. 4, 7 and 9) are digits that genuinely look alike, so their neighbourhoods overlap.

So how does t-SNE do this? Three steps: (1) measure neighbour similarities in the high-dimensional space, (2) define neighbour similarities in the low-dimensional map, (3) move the map points until the two sets of similarities match.

Section 5

5Inside t-SNE, Step 1: Turning Distance into "Neighbour Probability"

We need a score that says how likely it is that point j is a neighbour of point i. Close points should score high, far points should score near zero.

A first (failed) attempt: inverse distance

A tempting rule is "probability is proportional to 1/distance." Two problems appear:

  • At distance zero the value shoots to infinity — not a valid probability.
  • Near small distances, a tiny increase in distance causes a huge drop in score — too violent to be a stable neighbourhood measure.

Better: a Gaussian (bell curve) centred on each point

For point i, place a bell curve over it. A point j gets a similarity that decays smoothly with squared distance. It's smooth (no sudden jumps, no infinity at zero) and normalised (divided by the sum over all other points, so values for point i add up to 1 — real probabilities).

How bell-curve width (σ) shapes "closeness" interactive

A narrow σ only counts very close points as neighbours; a wide σ lets even far points count. Drag σ and watch the curve.

σ = 1.00

Why does each point get its own width, σᵢ?

Suppose point i sits in a dense region and point j in a sparse region. Take the same distance in both cases:

  • In the dense region, that distance is large compared with the typical neighbour spread — the other point is far away relative to the neighbourhood.
  • In the sparse region, the same distance is perfectly normal — the other point is part of the neighbourhood.

So the bell's width adapts to local density: narrow in dense regions, wide in sparse regions — distance measured relative to the cluster spread.

How is σᵢ chosen? Perplexity

You don't pick σᵢ directly. You pick perplexity — roughly "how many neighbours each point should consider." t-SNE searches for the σᵢ that gives each point that effective number of neighbours. The two directions are then averaged into one symmetric score so all pᵢⱼ sum to 1.

Experiment: the same distance, different neighbourhood density

A dense cluster (spread 0.31) and a sparse cluster (spread 1.37), each with an extra point placed exactly 1.0 unit from its centre — the same physical distance in both cases. How "neighbourly" does each one look?

Fixed σ vs. adaptive σ interactive · real results

Probability that the offset point (1.0 unit from the centre) counts as a neighbour — compared under one shared σ versus a σ matched to each cluster's own spread.

With one shared width, the offset point looks like a plausible neighbour of the dense cluster (0.0218) even though it's clearly far from a tightly packed group. With an adaptive width, the same point is judged against its own neighbourhood: in the dense cluster its probability collapses to almost zero (0.0004), while in the sparse cluster it stays high (0.0503). That's what a per-point σ buys.

Quick check

In the formula for p(j|i), the numerator is divided by the sum over all other points, and each point i has its own σᵢ. What do these two choices do?

Correct. Normalising makes the values for each point a proper probability distribution. A per-point σᵢ adapts to how crowded the surroundings are, so "close" means "close compared with my neighbours." These are not speed tricks, and they don't handle outliers, standardisation, or map overlap.
Section 6

6Inside t-SNE, Step 2: The Crowding Problem and the "t"

Now we need similarities in the low-dimensional map. A natural idea is to reuse the same Gaussian — it turns out that fails, because of the crowding problem.

The crowding problem

Imagine four points A, B, C, D in 2D, where A is at the centre and B, C, D are all equidistant from A and from each other. Squash them onto a 1D line: put A in the middle, B to its left — C must be as far from B as from A, and this fails. No matter where C goes, some distance is wrong.

There is simply not enough room in low dimensions. High-dimensional space has far more "room around a point":

How many mutually equidistant points fit? interactive

In d dimensions, you can fit d + 1 points that are all the same distance from each other. Slide to see how room shrinks as you compress into fewer dimensions.

2D

Points that are moderately far apart in high dimensions have nowhere to go in 2D, so they get crushed together in the middle, and clusters blur into one blob.

The fix: a heavy-tailed distribution in the low-dimensional space

Instead of a Gaussian, t-SNE uses a Student t-distribution with 1 degree of freedom in the map. The key difference is the tails: a Gaussian dies out extremely fast, while the t-distribution decays slowly.

  • Points that are moderately far apart in high-D are allowed to sit much farther apart in the map without being penalised.
  • That frees up space, so clusters push apart into separate islands instead of being crushed into one blob.
  • Close neighbours still stay close.

This is where the "t" in t-SNE comes from.

At distance 5, the Gaussian similarity is essentially zero, while the t-distribution still gives a small but real value. So in the map, two moderately-far clusters can be placed far apart at little cost — relieving the crowding.

Quick check

Which design choice in t-SNE directly addresses the crowding problem?

Correct. The heavy tails let moderately distant points sit far apart in 2D without a large penalty, giving crowded clusters room to separate. A Gaussian in both spaces is what causes crowding; infinite probabilities are invalid and unstable; perplexity controls neighbourhood size, not crowding.
Section 7

7Inside t-SNE, Step 3: Moving the Points

We now have two sets of numbers: pij (how neighbourly points i and j are in the original space — fixed) and qij (how neighbourly they are in the map — changes as points move). We want them to agree.

The mismatch is measured with the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. If pij ≈ qij everywhere, the divergence is ≈ 0 — the map respects the original neighbourhoods. It's large when two points are neighbours in high-D but far apart in the map — a heavy penalty for tearing neighbours apart. It's gentle the other way (non-neighbours placed close), which is why t-SNE cares more about local than global structure.

How the points move

Starting from random points in 2D, gradient descent nudges each point: where pij > qij (should be closer than they are), the term pulls i and j together; where pij < qij (too close), it pushes them apart.

The map is a system of springs where neighbours attract and non-neighbours repel, until it settles.

Two optimisation tricks make the sklearn defaults make sense: early exaggeration (the pij are multiplied by a constant in the first iterations, so tight clusters form early with empty space between them), and standard momentum and learning rate tools for convergence.

Build a mini t-SNE yourself (about 50 lines)

A bare-bones t-SNE that follows exactly the three steps above, run on 1,000 images of the small 8×8 sklearn digits dataset (64 features) so it finishes in seconds.

Show full code 
def _hbeta(D, beta):
    D = D - D.min()
    P = np.exp(-D * beta)
    sumP = P.sum()
    H = np.log(sumP) + beta * np.sum(D * P) / sumP
    return H, P / sumP

def high_dim_P(X, perplexity=30.0, tol=1e-5):
    n = X.shape[0]
    sq = (X ** 2).sum(1)
    D = np.maximum(sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * X @ X.T, 0)
    P = np.zeros((n, n))
    target = np.log(perplexity)
    for i in range(n):
        beta, lo, hi = 1.0, -np.inf, np.inf
        Di = np.delete(D[i], i)
        H, Pi = _hbeta(Di, beta)
        for _ in range(60):
            diff = H - target
            if abs(diff) < tol: break
            if diff > 0:
                lo = beta; beta = beta * 2 if hi == np.inf else (beta + hi) / 2
            else:
                hi = beta; beta = beta / 2 if lo == -np.inf else (beta + lo) / 2
            H, Pi = _hbeta(Di, beta)
        P[i, np.arange(n) != i] = Pi
    P = (P + P.T) / (2 * n)
    return np.maximum(P, 1e-12)

def tsne_from_scratch(X, perplexity=30, n_iter=500, lr=200, exaggeration=4, seed=0):
    n = X.shape[0]
    P = high_dim_P(X, perplexity)
    rng = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    Y = rng.randn(n, 2) * 1e-2
    update = np.zeros_like(Y)
    history = []
    for it in range(n_iter):
        ex = exaggeration if it < 100 else 1.0
        mom = 0.5 if it < 100 else 0.8
        sq = (Y ** 2).sum(1)
        num = 1.0 / (1.0 + sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * Y @ Y.T)
        np.fill_diagonal(num, 0)
        Q = np.maximum(num / num.sum(), 1e-12)
        PQ = (ex * P - Q) * num
        grad = 4 * (PQ.sum(1)[:, None] * Y - PQ @ Y)
        update = mom * update - lr * grad
        Y = Y + update
        Y -= Y.mean(0)
        if it % 50 == 0:
            history.append((it, np.sum(P * np.log(P / Q))))
    return Y, history

Section 8

8Hyperparameters: What Perplexity Does to Your Map

Perplexity is the main knob — roughly "how many neighbours each point considers."

  • It's a setting you choose. It is not calculated from the data, and it is not the size of any cluster.
  • A cluster can be much larger or smaller than the perplexity value — perplexity only controls how many neighbours each point pays attention to.

Perplexity explorer interactive

Click a value to see what it does to the map, run on 3,000 of Meera's digit scans.

The lesson: never trust one t-SNE picture. Try several perplexities, and trust only the structure that appears in all of them.

  • The number of iterations matters too. If t-SNE stops too early, the map hasn't settled — check the KL divergence: when it flattens, the map has stopped changing.
  • Re-run t-SNE with a few different iteration counts as well as a few perplexities. Trust a cluster only if its shape stays the same across all of these runs.
  • Even with the same perplexity and iteration count, results can vary slightly, because t-SNE starts from a random layout. Fixing random_state makes one run repeatable — it does not make that one layout the truth.

Quick check

Meera's t-SNE map shows island A much larger than island B, and the gap between A and C much wider than between A and B. The operations head concludes: "A is the most varied handwriting group, and C is the most different from A." Which conclusions are justified?

Correct — neither. t-SNE adapts to local density, so island size doesn't reflect how spread out the original group is, and gaps between islands don't reflect true distances. Both are artefacts of the method. What can be trusted: points in the same island are neighbours in the original space. Any claim about variation or dissimilarity needs to be checked in the original data.

Wrap-Up: Back to Meera's Desk

Meera presents her new map to the operations head.

  • The 4s, 7s and 9s that were piled on top of each other in the PCA plot are now separate islands, with visually similar digits sitting next to each other. The operations team now has the neighbourhood map it asked for.
  • She also arrives with a warning: island size and the gaps between islands should not be read as real distances, the map changes with perplexity and seed, and new scans cannot be dropped into an existing map.
  • For compact storage and model inputs, she keeps PCA. For the dashboard map, she uses t-SNE on PCA-reduced data.

Key takeaways

  • PCA captures global variance with a linear, reusable projection — it can destroy local neighbourhoods.
  • t-SNE keeps neighbours as neighbours. It does not preserve distances.
  • Step 1: turn high-D distances into neighbour probabilities, using a Gaussian whose width σᵢ adapts to local density (set via perplexity).
  • Step 2: use a heavy-tailed Student t in the map to fix the crowding problem.
  • Step 3: minimise the KL divergence by gradient descent, with neighbours attracting and non-neighbours repelling.
  • Read maps carefully: sizes and gaps are not meaningful, results vary by run, and there is no transform for new data.
  • Good workflow: standardise or scale, PCA to about 50 dimensions, then t-SNE, and compare several perplexities.
